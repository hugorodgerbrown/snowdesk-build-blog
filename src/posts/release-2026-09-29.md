Release `2026.09.29` went to production today. It holds twenty-three commits,
and most of them are maintenance: dependency bumps, the weekly map review
and page audit. Two stories are worth telling. A saved route now takes its
heights from the ground rather than from the device that recorded it. And
Snowdesk now draws its danger map inside Claude, which meant building an
OAuth server first.

## 1. A route's heights come from the terrain model

### The altimeter was lying

A GPX file's `<ele>` values come from the recording device's barometric
altimeter. Over a short span an altimeter tracks the shape of the ground
well. Over a morning it drifts with the weather.

That drift is worse than it sounds. Take one of our canonical test tracks,
Mont Fort – Backside. The recorded height starts 139 m above the terrain
model. By 1.5 km in, it is **more than 700 m** above the model, and the skier is
going *down* the whole time. Snowdesk cuts a route into legs (climbs and
descents), and on the device heights it read that stretch as a 215 m climb
followed by an 874 m drop. On the terrain model it is one descent. The
stored ascent for the whole track was 478.8 m; the model says 337.2 m.

Every figure built on those heights was wrong in the same way: the profile,
the legs, the ascent and descent totals, and the along-track gradient.

### The fix cost almost nothing

We already had the terrain model's heights to hand. To work out slope
angles, the slope sampler visits the 5 m terrain grid every 25 m along a
route. The height at each of those points is one more read from a tile
that is already in memory. The sampler now keeps that height alongside the
angle.

The new part is merging those 25 m sample points **into** the track, rather
than only reading heights at the vertices the file stored. A planned route
only has a vertex where its line turns. Read at the vertices alone, a
straight 1 km line over a hill reports no climb at all. With the sample
points merged in, the line climbs the hill.

Where the terrain model has no data (off the edge of the Swiss grid, for
example) the device height is still used, but it is **moved onto the
model's level first**. Each gap is shifted by the difference between device
and model measured at the known points either side of it. Otherwise an
altimeter that is 600 m out would put a 600 m cliff at the edge of the
model's coverage, and the totals would count it. Shifting the gap keeps the
shape the device recorded, which altimeters get right, and drops its
absolute level, which they get wrong.

### Why nothing stored was rewritten

The model's heights are applied when a route is read, not written back
over the uploaded track. There are three reasons:

1. **The upload is the user's.** Comparing the device heights with the
   model is also how we detect a recording fault, so the device series has
   to survive.
2. **The terrain grid will improve.** Resampling against a newer grid
   updates every figure without a data migration over user rows.
3. **It fails safe.** If a slope record can't be matched to the stored
   points, the route falls back to its stored track rather than pinning a
   height to the wrong patch of ground.

One rule, `climb_figures`, now produces ascent and descent for every
surface that shows them: the routes feed, both trip payloads, the route
row, the trip page and the past-trip row. They can no longer disagree with
each other.

## 2. The two route rails, redrawn

A saved route opens two strips at the foot of the map. We call them rails.
With the heights now trustworthy, this release reworked both.

**Rail one** is the route's header. It now reads as two lines: a vertical
line (ascent · descent) and a horizontal line (length · how much of the
route is on steep terrain).

**Rail two** is the terrain under one leg. With no leg open it no longer
sits blank. It says *Select a route leg to view terrain* and acts as the
leg picker, and one motion opens a leg. Drag along it and a readout
describes the ground in plain words (*Fall line*, *Ground falls away left*,
*Traverse · falls away right*) above the numbers (*37° slope · 36° bank*).
On a phone, a one-finger drag now reads a point, as the hint says it will;
two fingers pan and zoom.

The bank angle is how steeply the ground tilts across your line of travel
rather than along it. It is now drawn as **level-ski wedges**. Each wedge
is a short ground line tipped by the bank, with the uphill side solid and
the downhill side faint. You can read which way the slope falls away at a
glance, without a legend.

### A shadow on steep ground

One terrain mark has come back to the map itself. Where the ground under
the route is **40° or steeper**, a darker line now runs 4.5 px to the
downhill side of the route line. It sits downhill at every zoom level,
never crosses the line (it stops where the bank changes sign), and fades
with its leg. It isn't tappable and has no legend entry. It is there so
that the steepest stretches catch your eye on the overview, before you open
anything.

## 3. Snowdesk inside Claude

Snowdesk has run a read-only [MCP](https://modelcontextprotocol.io) server
for a while, so an assistant such as Claude can ask for current conditions
and danger-rating history instead of scraping bulletin pages. This release
adds its first **interactive view**: `show_danger_map`.

Ask Claude about today's avalanche danger in Switzerland and it can now
show a map right in the conversation, with each warning region coloured by
its peak EAWS danger rating. You can step between days, and clicking a
region tells the model which one you picked without starting a new turn.
From a region you can open its bulletin, jump to the Snowdesk map framed on
it, or ask a follow-up question.

<!-- screenshot pending: /assets/img/release-2026-09-29/danger-map-claude.png — The danger-map view rendered inside an MCP host -->

Two details took most of the work:

- **The coordinates never reach the model.** The region boundaries (about
  150 KB of Swiss coordinates) come from a second tool that only the view
  can call. The model gets ratings and names. The picture gets geometry.
- **It draws before it can draw properly.** The view uses MapLibre, and
  MapLibre starts a web worker from a `blob:` URL. The MCP Apps content
  security policy has no directive for workers, so a strict host refuses
  it. The view therefore draws an SVG version over plain raster tiles
  straight away: swisstopo's winter map when every region is Swiss,
  OpenStreetMap otherwise. It swaps MapLibre in only if MapLibre actually
  loads. A refused worker gives up at once, with an 8-second timeout as
  the backstop.

### So we wrote an OAuth server

Until this release the MCP endpoint was anonymous. The only control that
allows is a per-IP rate limit, and no call has an account behind it. Now
every call needs a bearer token that Snowdesk issues itself. You connect
Claude to your Snowdesk account once, and you can disconnect it from
`/account/settings/`.

We wrote the authorization server by hand, on plain Django, with no OAuth
library. That sounds like the wrong call, so here is the reasoning:

- **The account already lives here.** Sign-in, passkeys, verification and
  the settings page all exist. An external identity provider would add a
  second account system to link, not take work away.
- **We need a narrow slice, precisely.** MCP and Claude's connector
  requirements need authorization code with PKCE, refresh tokens, two
  discovery documents, client metadata documents (CIMD), dynamic client
  registration and token audiences. Some of the rules are exacting: the
  `resource` must echo the URL the user typed, path included. The libraries
  cover far more than this, don't support CIMD at all, and would have
  needed configuring around exactly the details Claude checks.
- **Small is auditable.** Four models and a few hundred lines of services
  keep every rule visible and tested. Only hashes of codes and tokens are
  stored. Access tokens live for an hour; refresh tokens live for thirty
  days and rotate on every use, and a reused refresh token is detected.

The cost is that security fixes to the flow are ours to make. Fetching a
client's metadata document means Snowdesk requests a URL the client
chose, so that fetch has its own SSRF guard. One quirk is worth passing on.
The site's content security policy says `form-action 'self'`, which blocks
a 302 redirect from the consent form back to the client. So after consent,
Snowdesk hands back to the client through a small meta-refresh page.
